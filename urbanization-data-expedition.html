Your Assignment

Briefing an organization that hasn't made up its mind yet

Around 80% of the world's population now lives in cities, towns, and suburbs. That wasn't always true — as recently as 1950, only about 20% of people lived in cities. But "urban" doesn't mean the same thing in every country, and how you define it can change the story the data seems to tell.

Imagine you've been hired as a junior research analyst for an international development organization. Your supervisor is drafting a policy briefing on whether rising urbanization should be treated as a sign of progress worth encouraging. Before it goes out, she's asked you to explore the actual data yourself — not just skim a summary — and come back with an evidence-based answer.

Getting started

Guiding Question
To what extent does becoming more urban automatically mean a country is more developed?
  • Interpret population and density trends using interactive data visualizations.
  • Compare urbanization patterns across regions and cities.
  • Evaluate the relationship between urbanization and development.

Density isn't the same as population

6. Which city is denser? Has the gap between them grown, shrunk, or stayed about the same over time?
From the page
"Hong Kong has around 7,000 people per square kilometer, while Karachi, in Pakistan, has over 27,000." The page also notes: "Hong Kong...continued to grow in population over the last decade without becoming more crowded. Karachi and Surat, by contrast, became steadily denser."
7. A city can grow in population without becoming more crowded, or become much more crowded without growing much in population. Which pattern shows up in the cities you looked at?
From the page
Shanghai and New Delhi are the page's own example: in the 1970s, both had around 6,500 people per km². By 2020, both reached similar population sizes, but New Delhi became about 50% more crowded — over 14,000 per km², compared to Shanghai's 9,500.

Life in the city isn't equal

Use the map to find one country in sub-Saharan Africa and one high-income country (try hovering over different regions).

8. What percentage did you find for each of your two countries? What does a "slum household" mean, according to the page (lacking clean water, sanitation, space, or durable housing)?
From the page
Globally, "around one in four people in urban areas live in slum households." In high-income countries the share is close to zero; across sub-Saharan Africa "more than half of urban residents live in slum households," and in Chad it's closer to 80%. A slum household lacks at least one of: clean water, sanitation, sufficient living space, or durable housing.
Stop 6 of 6 — Capstone

Does urbanization mean development?

Both charts below claim to show "urbanization vs. GDP." Look closely at both before answering.
Chart A: "Urbanization vs. GDP" (national definitions). Our World in Data, CC BY.
Chart B: "Share of population in urban areas vs. GDP per capita" (Degree of Urbanisation standard). Our World in Data, CC BY.
9. Both charts plot urbanization against GDP, but the relationship looks stronger in one than the other. Which one shows a stronger link? What's different about how each chart defines "urban"?
From the page
Chart A uses "each country's own national definition of 'urban.'" Chart B uses the standardized Degree of Urbanisation framework. The page explains: "national definitions undercount urbanization in poorer countries, which strengthens the apparent link" in Chart A. Using the same standard for every country in Chart B, "this distortion goes away" and the correlation "is much weaker."
10. Final answer: To what extent does becoming more urban automatically mean a country is more developed? Use at least one specific chart from today to support your answer.
From the page
The page's own answer is "to some extent, but less than you'd think, and not automatically." At the micro level, density brings real economic benefits (shared infrastructure, specialization, faster idea exchange), but the effect is "real, but modest." At the macro level, "countries that start out more urbanized do not consistently grow faster or slower than those that start out more rural." What matters more is how urbanization happens: driven by industrialization (much of East and Southeast Asia), it tends to bring real gains; driven by resource booms or "rural push" factors (parts of sub-Saharan Africa), it often happens "without comparable economic gains."
